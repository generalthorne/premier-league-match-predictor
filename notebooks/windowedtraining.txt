## Training Window Experiment — Results

I tested training windows ranging from 2 to 8 seasons to see how much historical data gives the best results. Each window was evaluated across the 2018–19 to 2024–25 seasons, using accuracy and log loss.

### Results

| Training window | Mean accuracy | Mean log loss | Overall accuracy | Overall log loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 seasons | 51.68% | 1.0152 | 51.65% | 1.0157 |
| 3 seasons | 52.02% | 1.0054 | 52.07% | 1.0058 |
| 4 seasons | 52.17% | 0.9952 | 52.14% | 0.9963 |
| 5 seasons | 51.41% | 0.9973 | 51.39% | 0.9981 |
| 6 seasons | **52.60%** | 0.9948 | **52.56%** | 0.9956 |
| 7 seasons | 51.88% | **0.9922** | 51.84% | **0.9931** |
| 8 seasons | 52.15% | 0.9926 | 52.14% | 0.9935 |

### What I took from this

- **6 seasons** produced the highest accuracy, at 52.60% mean accuracy.
- **7 seasons** produced the lowest log loss, at 0.9922 mean and 0.9931 overall.
- The 7-season window also had the lowest variation in log loss (0.0384), suggesting relatively consistent probability predictions across the evaluation seasons.
- The differences are fairly small, so this isn't proof that seven seasons is universally optimal.

### Final decision

I will use a **7-season training window** for the final model. Since the project predicts probabilities for home wins, draws, and away wins, log loss is particularly useful for evaluating how good those probabilities are.

For the final evaluation:
- Training: 2017–18 to 2023–24 (7 seasons, 2,660 matches)
- Validation: 2024–25 (380 matches)
- Testing: 2025–26 (380 matches)

This gives me a fixed setup for the final evaluation without using the test season to make further model decisions.
